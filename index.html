7 Detailed Modules

This extended study guide directly aligns with the 50 practice items below. Review these core historical concepts, constitutional foundations, foreign policy doctrines, and Supreme Court precedents prior to beginning your exam session.

1 Colonial Era, Independence & Constitutional Foundations (1776–1789)

• Declaration of Independence (1776): Written primarily by Thomas Jefferson. Grounded in John Locke’s Enlightenment philosophy of natural rights ("life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness") and the social contract (government derives authority from the consent of the governed).

• Articles of Confederation (1781–1789): America's first constitution. Intentionally created a weak national government with no executive branch, no national judiciary, and no power to levy taxes or regulate commerce.

• Shays' Rebellion (1786): An armed uprising of western Massachusetts farmers protesting debt and tax foreclosures. It exposed the central government's inability to maintain public order under the Articles, prompting the Constitutional Convention.

• Constitutional Compromises & Principles:

  • Great Compromise: Created a bicameral legislature—the Senate (equal representation: 2 per state) and the House of Representatives (proportional to population).
  • Federalism: The constitutional system that divides power between the national (federal) government and individual state governments.
  • Checks and Balances: Mechanisms ensuring no single branch dominates (e.g., Presidential veto over Congress, Congressional power to override vetoes, Supreme Court judicial review).

• The Bill of Rights (1791): The first 10 amendments protecting individual civil liberties. The First Amendment guarantees freedom of speech, religion, press, assembly, and petition.

2 Early Republic, Westward Expansion & Sectional Division (1800–1860)

• Executive Branch & Constitution: Article II establishes the Executive Branch headed by the President to enforce and execute federal laws.

• Louisiana Purchase (1803): President Thomas Jefferson purchased 828,000 square miles from France for $15 million, doubling the size of the U.S. and securing control of the Mississippi River.

• Monroe Doctrine (1823): President James Monroe declared the Western Hemisphere closed to future European colonization, warning foreign powers against interfering in American nations.

• Manifest Destiny: The 19th-century ideology holding that the U.S. was divinely ordained to expand across North America from the Atlantic to the Pacific.

• Sectional Crises over Slavery:

  • Missouri Compromise (1820): Admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state; prohibited slavery north of the 36°30' parallel in the Louisiana Territory.
  • Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854): Repealed the 36°30' line and introduced popular sovereignty (allowing voters in territories to decide on slavery), sparking violent conflict known as "Bleeding Kansas".

• Judicial Review: Established in Marbury v. Madison (1803), giving the Supreme Court authority to strike down laws that violate the U.S. Constitution.

3 Civil War & Reconstruction Era (1861–1877)

• Civil War (1861–1865): Stemmed from sectional conflicts over slavery, states' rights, and economic differences between the industrial North and agrarian South.

• Emancipation Proclamation (1863): Issued by Abraham Lincoln following the Battle of Antietam. Declared free all enslaved persons residing in states currently in rebellion against the Union.

Reconstruction Amendments (Crucial GED Knowledge):
13th Amendment (1865): Formally abolished slavery and involuntary servitude nationwide.
14th Amendment (1868): Granted citizenship to all persons born or naturalized in the U.S. and guaranteed Equal Protection and Due Process under the law.
15th Amendment (1870): Prohibited denying voting rights based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

• Reconstruction Era (1865–1877): Federal effort to rebuild the Southern economy, readmit former Confederate states, and protect African American rights. The Freedmen's Bureau provided food, medical care, and education to newly freed individuals.

• Compromise of 1877 & Jim Crow: The withdrawal of federal troops marked the end of Reconstruction, enabling Southern states to enact Jim Crow laws enforcing legal segregation and voter suppression.

4 Industrialization, Progressive Reforms & Imperialism (1870–1920)

• Second Industrial Revolution: Unprecedented growth in industrial production, railroads, and corporate monopolies. The Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) was passed to outlaw trusts and anti-competitive business practices.

• Progressive Era Reforms: Movements aimed at correcting industrial social ills. Investigative journalists called Muckrakers exposed corporate corruption and unsanitary conditions (e.g., Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle led to the Meat Inspection Act and Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906).

• Workplace Safety: Tragedies like the 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire spurred state and national labor safety regulations and mandatory building codes.

• Theodore Roosevelt's Square Deal: Domestic agenda focusing on consumer protection, control of corporations, and natural resource conservation.

• 19th Amendment (1920): Granted women the constitutional right to vote after decades of organized suffrage campaigning.

• Spanish-American War (1898): America emerged as an imperial power, gaining control of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines.

5 World War I, Great Depression & The New Deal (1914–1941)

• World War I (1914–1918): U.S. entered in 1917 following German unrestricted submarine warfare (e.g., sinking of the Lusitania) and the Zimmermann Telegram. President Woodrow Wilson proposed the Fourteen Points peace plan, but the Senate rejected joining the League of Nations to preserve U.S. isolationism.

• Great Depression (1929–1939): Sparked by the October 1929 stock market crash, bank panics, overproduction, and the Dust Bowl (severe ecological drought in the Great Plains). Unemployment hit 25%.

• FDR's New Deal: Comprehensive reform programs organized around Relief, Recovery, and Reform:

  • FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation): Insured individual bank deposits to prevent bank runs.
  • Social Security Act (1935): Created old-age pensions, disability benefits, and unemployment compensation.
6 World War II & Cold War Geopolitics (1939–1991)

• World War II (1939–1945): Ended U.S. isolationism after the surprise attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941.

• Executive Order 9066: Authorized the mandatory relocation and internment of over 120,000 Japanese Americans during WWII.

• Cold War Doctrines & Policies:

  • Containment: Strategic foreign policy designed to block the international spread of Soviet communism.
  • Truman Doctrine (1947): Pledged economic and military aid to nations resisting communist subversion.
  • Marshall Plan: Massive economic assistance program to rebuild Western European economies post-WWII.
  • Korean War (1950–1953): Cold War proxy conflict fought to stop North Korean communist invasion across the 38th Parallel.
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): 13-day tense nuclear confrontation between the U.S. and USSR over Soviet missiles in Cuba.
  • Fall of the Berlin Wall (1989): Symbolized the collapse of Eastern European communism and the impending end of the Cold War.
7 Civil Rights Movement, Modern Era & Landmark Supreme Court Cases

• Civil Rights Legislation & Milestones:

  • Civil Rights Act of 1964: Banned discrimination in public accommodations, employment, and federally funded programs.
  • Voting Rights Act of 1965: Outlawed discriminatory voting hurdles like literacy tests.
  • Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.: Led nonviolent protests; delivered "I Have a Dream" speech in 1963.
Essential Supreme Court Precedents:
Marbury v. Madison (1803): Established Judicial Review.
Plessy v. Ferguson (1896): Upheld segregation under "separate but equal".
Brown v. Board of Education (1954): Overturned Plessy; ruled public school segregation unconstitutional.
Miranda v. Arizona (1966): Ruled police must inform suspects of rights (silence, legal counsel) prior to interrogation.

• Watergate Scandal (1972–1974): Political break-in and cover-up that led directly to President Richard Nixon's resignation.

• Reaganomics: 1980s supply-side economic policy emphasizing tax cuts, deregulation, and reduced domestic spending.

• Department of Homeland Security: Created in 2002 to coordinate national counterterrorism efforts following the 9/11 attacks.

• Key Constitutional Concepts: Impeachment (House charges federal official with misconduct), Due Process (guarantee of legal fairness), Rule of Law (all individuals/officials are subject to law), and the 26th Amendment (1971) (lowered voting age to 18).
